AI adoption · 6 min read

Five questions to ask before you invest in AI

Every leadership team is being asked what it is doing about AI. The pressure to act is real, but the organisations getting value are the ones that start with the business problem, not the tool.

1. Which work actually hurts?

Look for high-volume, repetitive tasks where people spend time drafting, searching, summarising or re-keying information. These are where AI most reliably saves time.

2. Is the process stable enough to improve?

Automating a broken process just produces broken outcomes faster. If a process varies wildly between teams, simplify and standardise it first.

3. Where does your data live, and who can see it?

Tools such as Microsoft Copilot surface whatever a user already has access to. Poor permissions become visible very quickly. Tidy access before rollout, not after.

4. What does good look like?

Agree a small number of measures up front: hours saved, turnaround time, error rates or customer response times. Without them, AI becomes a cost line with anecdotes attached.

5. Who owns adoption?

Licences don't change behaviour; people do. Plan for training, champions, guidance on safe use and a feedback loop. Adoption is a change programme and needs to be run like one.

Delivery · 5 min read

Spotting a failing programme before it fails

When a transformation programme goes wrong, the post-mortem often reveals that people knew. The signals were there; they just weren't joined up or acted on.

The status report is always amber

A programme that has been "amber, recovering" for three reporting cycles is red. Persistent amber usually means the reporting is protecting someone rather than informing decisions.

Requirements keep growing, dates don't move

Scope creep without re-planning is borrowing from the future. Somewhere, quality or testing is being squeezed to make the numbers work.

The business has stopped turning up

When senior users stop attending design sessions and send delegates instead, the programme has lost its sponsor in all but name.

Nobody can explain the benefits

If the team cannot describe, in plain language, what will be better and for whom, the programme has drifted from its purpose.

An independent Transformation Health Check gives executives an honest view and a recovery plan while there is still time to use it.

Managed services · 5 min read

Why offshoring fails, and how to make it work

Many UK businesses have tried offshoring and been burned: missed deadlines, quality issues and a UK manager spending half their week chasing. The talent was rarely the problem. The model was.

Buying hours shifts the risk to you

When you rent people by the hour, every inefficiency is your cost and every quality issue is your problem to manage. The supplier is paid either way.

Define the outcome, not the headcount

Describe what "done" looks like: the reports, the documents processed, the turnaround time and the quality standard. Then let the supplier decide how to staff it.

Write the playbook first

Documented processes are what make a remote team effective, and they mean the knowledge belongs to you, not to whoever happens to be doing the work.

Put AI in the loop

AI handles the repetitive first pass; people review, correct and improve it. Costs fall over time instead of creeping up.

Cyber security · 4 min read

Cyber security is a business change programme

New controls, new tooling and new policies all change how people work. Yet security programmes are often scoped and resourced as if they were infrastructure upgrades.

Translate risk into business language

Boards fund what they understand. Framing security work in terms of service continuity, customer trust and regulatory exposure gets better decisions than framing it in terms of technical controls.

Map the processes you are changing

Access management, joiners-movers-leavers, incident response and supplier assurance are all business processes. Good business analysis makes sure the new way of working is clear, owned and workable.

Plan for adoption

Awareness, clear guidance and a sensible exceptions process stop people finding workarounds. A control nobody follows is not a control.
